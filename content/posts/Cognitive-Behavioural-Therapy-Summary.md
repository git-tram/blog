## Cognitive Behavioural Therapy Summary

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is a structured, time-limited form of psychotherapy that integrates principles from both cognitive therapy and behaviour therapy. It aims to change automatic thoughts, maladaptive behaviours, and underlying schemas to improve emotional experiences and overall functioning.

At the core of CBT is the cognitive model, which posits that dysfunctional thinking influences mood and behaviour, and that such thinking is common across all psychological disturbances. This model operates at three levels of cognition:

1. **Automatic Thoughts**  
    These are immediate, often unconscious thoughts that arise in response to specific situations. They are typically fleeting and situation-specific, and they directly influence emotions and behaviours. In CBT, the focus is on identifying these thoughts and evaluating their validity through cognitive restructuring techniques.
2. **Intermediate Beliefs**  
    These lie between automatic thoughts and core beliefs. They include attitudes, rules, and assumptions that shape how individuals interpret events and make decisions. Intermediate beliefs are more stable than automatic thoughts but less foundational than core beliefs. They are often unarticulated and may not be consciously recognized, yet they significantly influence a person’s thought patterns. Examples include rules such as “If someone ignores me, then I must be unlovable” or assumptions like “People only help those who are already strong.”
3. **Core Beliefs**  
    Core beliefs are deeply held, stable, and often unconscious ideas about the self, others, and the world. They are typically formed in early childhood and serve as the foundation for all other beliefs. These beliefs are generalised, global, and can be rigid or extreme. In CBT, core beliefs are considered the most influential layer of cognition, as they underlie and shape both automatic thoughts and intermediate beliefs. Common examples include:
    - "I am helpless"
    - "I am unlovable"
    - "I am unworthy"  
        These beliefs may be negative and maladaptive, leading to persistent emotional distress and dysfunctional behaviour.

The relationship between these levels is hierarchical: core beliefs influence the development of intermediate beliefs, which in turn shape automatic thoughts. This structure explains how a single event can trigger a cascade of negative thoughts and emotional responses, especially when the underlying core belief is negative or distorted

CBT is guided by several key principles:
- Treatment plans are built on an evolving cognitive conceptualisation of the client’s issues.
- It identifies behavioural obstacles, precipitating factors, key developmental events, and enduring patterns of interpretation.
- A strong therapeutic relationship is essential for effective treatment.
- Progress is continuously monitored throughout therapy.
- CBT is culturally adapted and tailored to the individual.
- It emphasises positive outcomes and active, directive, yet collaborative treatment.

CBT is grounded in the idea that behaviour is determined not only by environmental contingencies but also by cognitive representations of events. It blends behaviour therapy (which focuses on modifying environmental stimuli) with cognitive therapy (which emphasises how individuals interpret and perceive the world).

Basic assumptions of CBT include:

- Overt behaviour holds primacy in shaping outcomes.
- Maladaptive behaviours are learned in the same way as adaptive ones.
- Assessment and evaluation are central to both diagnosis and treatment.
- Treatment is active, directive, and involves some level of collaboration between therapist and client.

Despite its effectiveness, CBT has limitations:

- It may lack insight into the deeper causes of psychological distress, as behaviour therapy tends to treat symptoms rather than root causes.
- The therapeutic process may involve a sense of control or manipulation by the therapist.
- Treatment is typically brief and focused on practical, empirically supported techniques.
